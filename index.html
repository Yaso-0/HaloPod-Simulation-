About This Project

HaloPod is a digital engineering and computational research project investigating whether a biodegradable multilayer capsule could improve the local microenvironment around mangrove propagules during early establishment in saline coastal sites.

This platform provides interactive tools to explore the capsule design, run diffusion simulations, and analyse model outputs. All parameter values are illustrative assumptions — not experimentally measured.

The Problem: Early Mangrove Establishment

Mangrove propagules face serious challenges during their first weeks in saline coastal environments.

1. Salinity Stress

High or fluctuating salinity can affect water uptake, osmotic balance, germination, and early growth. Dissolved salts — mainly Na⁺ and Cl⁻ ions — create osmotic stress that makes it harder for plant cells to absorb water. At high concentrations, specific ions can also cause ion toxicity, damaging cellular structures and enzymes.

Osmotic stress: When external salt concentration is high, the water potential outside the plant is lower than inside, reducing the plant's ability to take up water.

2. Physical Displacement

Waves, tidal currents, and sediment movement can dislodge propagules before their roots have time to anchor into the substrate. This is one of the main reasons why direct planting in exposed coastal sites has variable success rates.

Blue Carbon Significance

Mangrove ecosystems are among the most carbon-dense habitats on Earth, storing 3–5 times more carbon per hectare than typical terrestrial forests. This "blue carbon" is locked in biomass and especially in deep sediment layers for centuries. Mangrove loss releases this carbon, while successful restoration can rebuild these carbon sinks.

Scientific Background

Established principles that guide the HaloPod design. Each principle is supported on its own — their combined effect in one capsule remains to be tested.

Mangrove Salt Adaptation

Mangroves are salt-adapted trees, but tolerance varies by species and growth stage. Avicennia marina secretes salt through leaf glands and can tolerate salinities above 35 ppt. Rhizophora stylosa uses an ultrafiltration mechanism at the root level to exclude salt.

During early establishment (germination and seedling stages), propagules may be more sensitive to salinity extremes than mature trees.

Biochar

Biochar is a porous carbon material produced by heating organic matter under limited oxygen (pyrolysis). Its properties — porosity, surface area, water-holding capacity — depend on feedstock type and pyrolysis temperature.

In HaloPod, biochar is proposed as a structural base layer that may also contribute to water retention and provide mechanical stability in sediment.

Alginate–Chitosan Hydrogel

Sodium alginate crosslinked with Ca²⁺ ions forms a hydrogel matrix. When coated with chitosan, the oppositely charged groups (COO⁻ from alginate, NH₃⁺ from chitosan) can form a polyelectrolyte complex.

Hydrogel swelling (water uptake) depends on ionic strength, pH, composition, and crosslinking density. In saline environments, swelling typically decreases as salt concentration increases.

Hydrogel Swelling

The hydrogel network expands by absorbing water. The degree of swelling depends on the balance between osmotic pressure (driving water in) and the elastic restoring force of the polymer network.

In saline water, the difference in osmotic pressure between inside and outside the gel is reduced, which typically leads to less swelling compared to fresh water. This is a salinity-responsive behaviour.

Ion Diffusion — The Mathematical Foundation

The transport of dissolved ions (like Na⁺ and Cl⁻) through materials is described by Fick's Laws of Diffusion.

Fick's First Law — Diffusion Flux

$$J = -D \frac{\partial C}{\partial x}$$

The flux \(J\) (amount of substance crossing a unit area per unit time) is proportional to the concentration gradient. The negative sign means diffusion goes from high to low concentration.

Fick's Second Law — Concentration Change Over Time

$$\frac{\partial C}{\partial t} = D \frac{\partial^2 C}{\partial x^2}$$

For a material with a single constant diffusion coefficient \(D\), this describes how concentration changes at each point over time.

Conservative Form — For Layered Materials

$$\frac{\partial C}{\partial t} = \frac{\partial}{\partial x}\left[D(x)\frac{\partial C}{\partial x}\right]$$

When the diffusion coefficient \(D\) varies with position (as in a multilayer capsule), the conservative form ensures that flux is properly conserved across material interfaces.

Engineering Design

HaloPod: a proposed biodegradable three-layer bio-capsule

Chitosan Outer Layer

A thin semi-permeable coating made of chitosan, a biopolymer derived from chitin. Chitosan carries positive charges (NH₃⁺) that can interact with the negatively charged alginate (COO⁻) underneath, forming a polyelectrolyte complex. This layer may influence ion selectivity and transport rate.

Ca²⁺-Crosslinked Alginate Hydrogel

The main matrix is sodium alginate crosslinked with calcium ions (Ca²⁺). This creates a hydrogel network that absorbs water and swells. Swelling behaviour is responsive to salinity — in saltier water, the gel typically swells less. This is the primary layer studied as a potential ion transport modifier.

Biochar Structural Base

A porous carbon layer produced by pyrolysis. Provides structural support and may add mass for sediment stability (to be tested). The pore network can hold water and may interact with ions. Properties depend on feedstock and production temperature.

Propagule Chamber

The biological core where the mangrove propagule is housed along with essential nutrients. The chamber positions and supports the propagule while the surrounding layers modulate the local environment.

Design principle: The shell is not a perfect salt barrier. It is studied as a material that may influence water and ion transport. Layer functions are design objectives that require material, mechanical, and biological testing.
Proposed future addition: Nanocellulose reinforcement of the hydrogel network is considered for future versions but is not part of the current design. Plant growth-promoting bacteria (PGPB) are also a potential optional addition.

Model Assumptions & Limitations

  • 1D simplification of a 3D capsule geometry.
  • Constant diffusion coefficients — no concentration-dependent or time-dependent D.
  • No hydrogel swelling dynamics modeled.
  • No biodegradation over time.
  • No biological interaction (germination, growth) modeled.
  • Illustrative parameters only — not experimentally validated.
  • Concentration is normalized (0–1), not actual ion concentration.
  • Cannot predict water retention from diffusion simulation alone.

What the Model Indicates

  • Layers with lower effective diffusion coefficients slow the advance of the concentration front toward the propagule chamber.
  • Thicker barrier layers increase the total diffusion delay.
  • The biochar composite layer, when modeled with a lower D than the hydrogel, adds an additional barrier effect.
  • The control scenario (no capsule) equilibrates almost immediately with the external environment.

What the Model Cannot Establish

  • Whether the modeled diffusion delay is sufficient to benefit actual propagule germination.
  • Whether the illustrative D values reflect the real material properties.
  • How hydrogel swelling changes D over time and salinity.
  • Germination rates, seedling survival, root development, or biomass.
  • Structural integrity, displacement resistance, or biodegradation timeline.
  • Water retention — this requires separate swelling and water-uptake experiments.
